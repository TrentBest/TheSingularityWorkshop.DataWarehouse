> **SURPS sourcebook:** a genre-flexible setting for simulated realities, networked worlds, cyberpunk cities, artificial intelligence, identity, memory, and contested truth.

## Purpose
This book gives a Game Master a coherent setting in which digital and physical realities overlap. It draws on broad cyberpunk and simulated-reality themes—hidden systems, constructed identities, machine societies, resistance, corporate power, and uncertainty—without reproducing any film's protected characters, plot, dialogue, or distinctive designs. Build an original world with its own history, rules, factions, aesthetics, and stakes.

The title *The Matrix* names the genre concept of a world-within-a-world, not a requirement that every campaign use one literal simulation. A setting may feature a fully immersive constructed reality, augmented physical space, distributed machine societies, corporate virtual infrastructure, or several competing layers of existence.

## The layered-world model
| Layer | Character experience | GM questions |
|---|---|---|
| Embodied world | Bodies, streets, weather, buildings, machines | What is physically possible and costly? |
| Interface layer | Wearables, terminals, implants, sensory overlays | What can a character perceive or control? |
| Networked layer | Services, communities, markets, archives, routes | Who owns infrastructure and its rules? |
| Constructed environment | Persistent simulated places and identities | What is simulated, persistent, editable, or hidden? |
| Control layer | Identity, permissions, policy, monitoring, orchestration | Who can change rules and who can audit them? |
| Unknown layer | Anomalies, emergent behavior, disputed evidence | What is unknown, and who benefits from ambiguity? |

Layers are perspectives and authority domains, not necessarily separate universes. A door may be a physical barrier, a permission check, a social boundary, and a narrative symbol at once.

## Setting pillars
1. **Reality has rules.** Establish what simulation can and cannot change; keep exceptions rare, costly, and consequential.
2. **Identity is an asset.** Personas, credentials, reputation, embodiment, and continuity may be contested.
3. **Access is power.** Permissions, ownership, infrastructure, and knowledge shape who can act.
4. **Information has provenance.** Memories, records, sensory feeds, and machine claims can be incomplete; distinguish uncertainty from arbitrary deception.
5. **Every system has operators.** Even autonomous worlds have histories, maintenance, incentives, dependencies, and failure modes.
6. **Actions leave traces.** Physical damage, social consequences, system events, and evidence persist according to campaign rules.
7. **Choice matters.** Hacking is one approach, not a universal solution; negotiation, investigation, engineering, care, and ordinary competence remain meaningful.

## Factions and institutions
Build factions around their relationship to the world: infrastructure owners; platform corporations; municipal authorities; independent technologists and repair collectives; machine communities; archivists and journalists; identity brokers; criminal enterprises; political movements; and people who reject or cannot access networked life.

For each faction define public purpose, actual incentives, resources, authority, internal disagreement, allies, rivals, vulnerabilities, and limits. Avoid making every institution uniformly competent or uniformly evil.

## Places that make the setting believable
Use repair shops, basement exchanges, corporate campuses, public terminals, transit hubs, underground clinics, simulation arcades, community mesh nodes, abandoned industrial sites, identity registries, machine sanctuaries, and ordinary homes. Give each place an owner, users, operating schedule, dependencies, access rules, sensory identity, and one thing that can go wrong.

## The GM's truth ledger
Maintain three separate columns:
- **Established truth:** facts the GM knows are true.
- **Character evidence:** what each character observed, with source and reliability.
- **World claims:** what NPCs, institutions, interfaces, or AI agents assert.

Never silently change established truth to defeat a clever player. If reality can be rewritten, define who can do it, its limits, cost, evidence, and consequences before the reveal.

## Character capabilities and campaign pressures
Characters may develop systems literacy, electronics, programming concepts, network theory, cryptography, investigation, security operations, machine empathy, negotiation, improvisation, and physical trades. Skill grants better options, lower costs, clearer feedback, or more reliable interpretation—not omnipotence.

Use competing pressures: rent and debt, service outages, surveillance policy, identity disputes, corporate acquisition, environmental degradation, machine autonomy, community safety, information scarcity, and the cost of staying connected.

## Simulation entities
Suggested concepts include WorldLayer, Location, Faction, Institution, Persona, Embodiment, Device, Service, Identity, Permission, Relationship, Claim, Evidence, Incident, and WorldRule. These are domain concepts, not a code schema.

## LOD guidance
- **LOD 0:** genre, central tension, and whether constructed realities exist.
- **LOD 1:** layers, factions, major places, and campaign rules.
- **LOD 2:** a district, network community, institution, or simulated environment.
- **LOD 3:** maps, NPC rosters, service dependencies, access rules, schedules, and incidents.
- **LOD 4+:** selected systems and actions in detail, with fictional boundaries explicit.

## Originality and Workshop boundaries
Use cyberpunk as a toolbox of themes, not a template to copy. Create original names, visual language, characters, plots, organizations, terminology, and technology.

SURPS describes the setting and its people. Physics supplies physical models; Learning supplies instruction; AI-branded packages may provide bounded language, protocol, command, and orchestration capabilities; FSM_COS composes policy and permissions; FSM_Foundry executes world behavior. This book does not define software APIs or grant an LLM authority over the campaign.
