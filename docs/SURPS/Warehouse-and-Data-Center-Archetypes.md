**SURPS sourcebook · Facility patterns, operating cultures, and scenario design**

## 1. Purpose

This catalogue moves from an abstract statement — “the settlement has a granary” or “the colony has a data center” — to a facility with a recognizable purpose, operating logic, people, dependencies, and story potential.

An archetype is a reusable pattern, not a mandatory blueprint. A site may combine several archetypes, contradict their usual assumptions, or evolve from one into another. The entries span historical periods, technological regimes, and environments; they do not imply that every culture follows the same path.

Use this catalogue with [The Warehouse Across Time and Space](The-Warehouse-Across-Time-and-Space.md), [The Data Center Across Time and Space](The-Data-Center-Across-Time-and-Space.md), and [Storage Environments and Extreme Facilities](Storage-Environments-and-Extreme-Facilities.md).

## 2. How to read an archetype

When expanding an archetype, establish its purpose, custody, contents or service, intake and output flows, dependencies, failure modes, consequences, and relevant LOD. These details are prompts rather than universal facts.

## 3. Physical warehouse archetypes

### 3.1 Household reserve
**Setting:** Any society with seasonal or uncertain supply. **Function:** Preserve essentials between harvests, journeys, income, or supply opportunities. **Contents:** Food, fuel, tools, clothing, seed, medicines, water, valuables. **Operating character:** Personal knowledge, containers, hiding places, family routines, and trust substitute for formal inventory systems. **Pressures:** Spoilage, pests, damp, fire, theft, hidden depletion, and scarcity. **Story hooks:** A winter reserve is contaminated; a family cache contains an inherited object; a missing store reveals a hidden dependent.

### 3.2 Village granary and communal reserve
**Setting:** Agricultural settlements, frontier communities, isolated habitats. **Function:** Buffer seasonal shortages and support collective survival. **Contents:** Grain, seed, dried food, tools, emergency fuel. **Operating character:** Contributions and withdrawals are governed by custom, stewards, religious authority, local law, or coercion. **Pressures:** Unequal contribution, inaccurate measures, pests, poor rotation, political capture, disputed allocation. **Story hooks:** The reserve is lower than the ledger claims; drought forces rationing; a neighboring settlement requests aid.

### 3.3 Temple, palace, or state storehouse
**Setting:** City-states, kingdoms, empires, theocracies. **Function:** Collect, account for, preserve, and redistribute resources under institutional authority. **Contents:** Tax grain, tribute, tools, textiles, precious goods, offerings, records, weapons. **Operating character:** Seals, tallies, scribes, guards, standardized containers, and formal offices establish custody. Storage is also political power. **Pressures:** Corruption, extraction, falsified accounts, succession crises, public resentment, siege. **Story hooks:** Two inventories disagree; a sealed chamber opens after a ruler's death; famine turns the storehouse into a legitimacy crisis.

### 3.4 Caravanserai and route depot
**Setting:** Trade routes, deserts, mountain passes, steppe corridors. **Function:** Support travelers, animals, merchants, and cargo between settlements. **Contents:** Feed, water, fodder, spare tack, tools, trade goods, fuel, messages. **Operating character:** Value comes from location, reliable service, security, and knowledge of the next route leg. **Pressures:** Water scarcity, banditry, route closure, animal disease, weather, delayed caravans. **Story hooks:** A caravan arrives without its guards; the only safe route is closed; the water reserve becomes a bargaining instrument.

### 3.5 Port, river, and transshipment warehouse
**Setting:** River civilizations, maritime trade, canals, industrial ports. **Function:** Hold cargo while it changes carrier, owner, route, or legal status. **Contents:** Bulk commodities, containers, provisions, ship stores, customs-controlled goods. **Operating character:** Loading geometry, tides, river levels, cranes, manifests, customs, and schedules determine throughput. **Pressures:** Storms, fire, congestion, pilferage, spoilage, blockade, labor stoppages. **Story hooks:** A manifest does not match its cargo; a critical shipment is trapped in customs; the harbor must be evacuated.

### 3.6 Armory and military quartermaster depot
**Setting:** Fortresses, armies, fleets, militias, expeditionary forces. **Function:** Maintain readiness by storing and issuing equipment and supplies. **Contents:** Weapons, armor, ammunition, uniforms, rations, medical supplies, tools, spare parts, fuel. **Operating character:** Issue authority, inspection, maintenance, compatibility, and accountability matter as much as quantity. **Pressures:** Sabotage, theft, obsolete equipment, unsafe storage, supply interruption, conflicting command priorities. **Story hooks:** A unit receives the wrong equipment; an inventory discrepancy suggests diversion; scarce supplies force a command decision.

### 3.7 Guild, merchant, and bonded warehouse
**Setting:** Commercial towns, trade leagues, regulated ports. **Function:** Safeguard goods whose ownership, taxation, or release is conditional. **Contents:** Raw materials, finished goods, imported cargo, valuable stock. **Operating character:** Custody may be separate from ownership; contracts, receipts, seals, liens, insurance, and brokers govern claims. **Pressures:** Fraud, forged documents, disputed ownership, insolvency, theft, tariff changes. **Story hooks:** Two parties hold claims to one consignment; the keeper knows a shipment's true origin; a creditor seeks seizure.

### 3.8 Cold store and biological repository
**Setting:** From ice houses and root cellars to modern refrigeration and biobanks. **Function:** Preserve perishable or living materials through environmental control. **Contents:** Food, medicines, seed, tissue, cultures, specimens. **Operating character:** Temperature, humidity, sanitation, power, monitoring, rotation, and custody are integral to the asset. **Pressures:** Power failure, coolant loss, contamination, equipment drift, biological change. **Story hooks:** Cooling fails slowly; a sample's provenance is challenged; a storm isolates the facility.

### 3.9 Industrial parts and maintenance store
**Setting:** Mines, factories, railways, shipyards, power stations, engineered habitats. **Function:** Keep infrastructure operating through stocked components and consumables. **Contents:** Bearings, motors, filters, tools, fasteners, cables, lubricants, control modules. **Operating character:** Compatibility and lead time can matter more than purchase price; one small component can stop a large system. **Pressures:** Obsolescence, undocumented substitutions, counterfeit parts, procurement delays, poor cataloguing. **Story hooks:** An irreplaceable component is missing; an old machine uses an unavailable part; emergency stock was cannibalized.

### 3.10 Automated fulfillment center
**Setting:** Modern and future high-volume commerce or institutional distribution. **Function:** Receive, identify, store, pick, pack, and dispatch many orders. **Contents:** Diverse packaged goods sorted by handling, demand, dimensions, and routing. **Operating character:** Conveyors, robots, scanners, software, people, docks, and transport schedules form one coordinated system. **Pressures:** Software faults, jams, sensor errors, labor disruption, network outage, cascading demand. **Story hooks:** Goods keep moving after inventory authority is lost; a routing fault cascades; manual fallback must be improvised.

### 3.11 Deep vault, fortress store, and dwarven hold
**Setting:** Mountain strongholds, subterranean civilizations, siege economies. **Function:** Preserve critical resources behind defensible, environmentally stable boundaries. **Contents:** Food, water, ore, tools, seed, records, heirlooms, weapons, machine components. **Operating character:** Gates, ventilation, drainage, structural stability, and internal transport shape the facility. It may be integral to the fortress's political and defensive design. **Pressures:** Entrapment, blocked shafts, groundwater, ventilation failure, factional control, siege depletion. **Story hooks:** An internal passage is compromised; a sealed lower store has unknown contents; sheltering more people consumes the strategic reserve.

### 3.12 Mobile cache network
**Setting:** Nomadic cultures, explorers, resistance movements, migratory fleets. **Function:** Distribute supplies across routes without relying on one permanent warehouse. **Contents:** Food, water, tools, medicine, fuel, communications gear. **Operating character:** Caches are found through maps, memory, signals, coded marks, or trusted custodians. **Pressures:** Lost coordinates, environmental change, adversary discovery, depletion, false markers. **Story hooks:** A cache is empty but recently disturbed; a route depends on a forgotten reserve; an adversary learned the signaling convention.

## 4. Information-infrastructure archetypes

### 4.1 Living archive and oral knowledge house
**Setting:** Societies where trained people preserve law, history, navigation, medicine, genealogy, or technical knowledge. **Function:** Maintain knowledge through memory, recitation, apprenticeship, and correction. **Operating character:** Redundancy comes from multiple custodians and repeated transmission; trust depends on lineage, reputation, ritual, and validation. **Pressures:** Death, displacement, language loss, persecution, deliberate alteration. **Story hooks:** The last custodian is missing; two lineages preserve incompatible accounts; a community debates what can be shared.

### 4.2 Scriptorium, library, and civic archive
**Setting:** Temples, monasteries, courts, universities, cities. **Function:** Preserve, copy, organize, interpret, and provide access to recorded knowledge. **Contents:** Tablets, scrolls, codices, maps, registers, catalogues, instruments. **Operating character:** Readers, copyists, cataloguers, preservation, access control, and provenance. **Pressures:** Fire, damp, censorship, theft, miscataloguing, translation error, loss of originals. **Story hooks:** A catalogue references a missing volume; a copy contains a suppressed passage; evacuation priorities are contested.

### 4.3 Mechanical calculation bureau
**Setting:** Industrializing societies, navigation offices, observatories, engineering bureaus, alternate histories. **Function:** Perform repeatable calculations with human operators and mechanical or electromechanical devices. **Operating character:** Standard procedures, calibration, maintenance, trained operators, and retained intermediate records. **Pressures:** Wear, drift, power interruption, operator error, incompatible standards. **Story hooks:** A calculation contradicts the official table; the only calibrated machine fails before a deadline; an old punched-card archive contains a hidden sequence.

### 4.4 Telegraph, telephone, and exchange station
**Setting:** Long-distance communication networks and speculative equivalents. **Function:** Route messages and connect distant institutions. **Operating character:** A network node shaped by physical routes, switching capacity, protocol, and operators. **Pressures:** Cable breaks, congestion, power loss, interception, routing error, weather, isolation. **Story hooks:** A station receives an impossible message; a remote line carries a repeating signal; emergency traffic must be prioritized.

### 4.5 Institutional mainframe center
**Setting:** Government, university, financial, scientific, and corporate institutions. **Function:** Provide centralized computation, records, and shared digital services. **Operating character:** Specialist staff schedule work, maintain hardware, control access, back up media, and coordinate service windows. **Pressures:** Resource contention, operator error, media failure, power and cooling dependencies, vendor lock-in. **Story hooks:** A batch job contains the only surviving result; maintenance overlaps an emergency; the system's documented owner no longer exists.

### 4.6 Colocation and hyperscale campus
**Setting:** Networked societies with large commercial or institutional computing demand. **Function:** Host compute and storage for many customers or enormous shared services. **Operating character:** An industrial utility constrained by power, heat removal, network access, land, water, equipment supply, and expertise. **Pressures:** Grid constraints, cooling failure, supply-chain concentration, regional hazards, interconnection failure. **Story hooks:** Regional outage forces workload priorities; tenant isolation fails at a network boundary; expansion is blocked by power allocation.

### 4.7 Edge station and remote outpost
**Setting:** Rural networks, industrial sites, ships, expeditions, disaster zones, planetary settlements. **Function:** Provide local computation, storage, control, or communication close to users and instruments. **Operating character:** Small, rugged, often unattended, and expected to continue while disconnected. **Pressures:** Limited power, heat, spare parts, physical access, bandwidth, delayed maintenance. **Story hooks:** Disconnection exceeds the autonomy plan; local records conflict with central authority; repair requires a part held elsewhere.

### 4.8 Scientific data observatory
**Setting:** Astronomy, particle physics, climate science, oceanography, medicine, sensor networks. **Function:** Ingest, preserve, process, and distribute observations and research. **Contents:** Raw measurements, calibration data, metadata, simulations, derived datasets, reproducibility records. **Operating character:** Provenance and scientific integrity are essential; storage is coupled to instruments and workflows. **Pressures:** Irreplaceable observations, sensor drift, corrupted metadata, extreme data rates, format obsolescence. **Story hooks:** A unique observation is disputed; calibration history is missing; a discovery depends on an old dataset.

### 4.9 Distributed planetary archive
**Setting:** Planetary civilizations, global sensor networks, resilient public infrastructure. **Function:** Preserve knowledge and provide services across separated facilities. **Operating character:** No single site is assumed reachable; replication, authority, consistency, jurisdiction, and recovery matter. **Pressures:** Network partitions, incompatible regional records, coordinated compromise, uneven energy, political fragmentation. **Story hooks:** Regions preserve incompatible histories; a surviving node holds an unverified copy; trust must be rebuilt after isolation.

### 4.10 Orbital, lunar, and interplanetary knowledge core
**Setting:** Space stations, lunar settlements, Mars habitats, probes, generation ships, interstellar expeditions. **Function:** Preserve mission-critical knowledge and provide local computation where communication is delayed or impossible. **Contents:** Navigation, engineering, medical and ecological knowledge, mission logs, science, fabrication instructions, cultural archives. **Operating character:** Autonomy and continuity matter more than central oversight; the facility may need to survive generations. **Pressures:** Radiation, thermal cycling, micrometeoroids, power degradation, media obsolescence, limited repair, institutional drift. **Story hooks:** A ship's archive contradicts its mission history; a lunar node has acted without contact; repair instructions are in a damaged format.

## 5. Cross-archetype design patterns

- **Reserve versus flow-through:** A reserve holds resources for future need; a flow-through node transfers goods or information efficiently. Preservation and throughput can conflict.
- **Centralized versus distributed custody:** Centralization concentrates expertise and coordination but creates critical dependencies. Distribution improves locality and isolation tolerance but requires local authority and reconciliation.
- **Visible versus hidden inventory:** Public stores can demonstrate legitimacy; hidden caches can preserve resistance. Transparency and secrecy each create benefits and risks.
- **Passive versus active preservation:** A sealed vault may rely on stable geology; cold stores and electronic facilities depend on active systems and maintenance.
- **Custody versus ownership:** The keeper of goods may not own them; the operator of a data center may not own hosted data. This distinction creates duties and disputes.

## 6. Reusable facility profile

Promote an archetype into a named site using this profile:

- **Identity:** name, local name, aliases, cultural meaning.
- **Archetype mix:** primary and secondary patterns.
- **Location:** terrain, climate, access, hazards, strategic position.
- **Purpose:** why it exists and whom it serves.
- **Ownership and custody:** owner, operator, custodian, authority.
- **Contents or services:** categories, quantities, criticality, restrictions.
- **Anatomy:** zones, routes, equipment, interfaces, boundaries.
- **Operations:** intake, classification, preservation, retrieval, dispatch, audit, recovery.
- **People:** roles, staffing, skills, schedules, internal culture.
- **Dependencies:** transport, energy, water, communications, suppliers, tools, institutions.
- **Capacity:** usable capacity, throughput, reserve, bottlenecks, growth.
- **Trust:** access, accountability, provenance, adversarial exposure.
- **Failure model:** routine degradation, acute incidents, cascading failure, recovery.
- **Scenario hooks:** conflicts, events, measurable state, meaningful choices.
- **LOD declaration:** detail present and detail intentionally abstracted.

## 7. LOD application

- **LOD 0:** name the function — reserve, depot, archive, exchange, data center.
- **LOD 1:** select an archetype, purpose, scale, owner, constituency, and major dependency.
- **LOD 2:** combine archetypes; define operating culture, anatomy, staff, contents, flow, and failure patterns.
- **LOD 3+:** specify the site, floor plan, zones, routes, inventories, equipment, people, schedules, records, and live incidents.

A high-LOD facility should be coherent, not merely crowded with detail. Every subsystem should have a purpose, an owner, a dependency, and a failure consequence.

## 8. Simulation boundary

Candidate entities include facility, zone, inventory lot, container, custodian, owner, access rule, route, equipment, service, dependency, incident, and maintenance task. Candidate events include receive, inspect, store, retrieve, transfer, replicate, isolate, fail, restore, audit, and decommission.

A SURPS implementation may choose which to model. This book does not claim that the DataWarehouse library implements a facility simulator, logistics engine, database, security system, or distributed data center.

## 9. Relationship to the software repository

The DataWarehouse library remains a low-level fixed-record memory component. Its DataLake, DataShelf, slot, handle, and lifecycle concepts describe its own contract; they are not a universal schema for warehouses, archives, or data centers. Domain archetypes must not impose software dependencies or dictate the library's memory architecture.
