> **SURPS sourcebook:** a practical construction method for coherent missions, encounters, factions, clues, consequences, and AI-assisted play.

## The scenario promise
A believable scenario is a small working world, not a sequence of arbitrary obstacles. It has people with goals, places with operations, systems with dependencies, information with provenance, and events that continue whether or not the players look at them.

## The scenario packet
Every substantial scenario should define: premise; established truth; actors and motives; places and operating details; assets and dependencies; clues and their reliability; pressure clocks; multiple approaches; consequences; and possible resolutions. Include safety boundaries and which AI assistance is permitted.

## Causal scenario design
Build a causal chain before writing scenes: motive → opportunity → action → system or social effect → observable evidence → response → consequence. Each link needs a plausible mechanism. A clue points toward a fact without automatically explaining it.

Create at least three routes to important information. Do not make one required skill check the only path forward. A failed roll should change cost, time, risk, or information quality rather than stop the story.

## Scene construction
A scene needs an immediate question, visible situation, active actors, available choices, stakes, and a state change. Describe what characters can perceive before asking for a check. Use sensory details that arise from the place: fan noise, stale air, failing lights, interface lag, vibration, crowd behavior, heat, signage, or inconsistent records.

## Operational realism checklist
For every organization ask: who owns the place or service; who is on duty and what can they authorize; what routine work happens; what does the system depend on; what is monitored and by whom; what is the maintenance history; what happens when a component or person is unavailable; what records are created; and what would a reasonable operator do under pressure?

## GM clocks and event schedules
Use clocks for developing conditions, not hidden punishments. Examples: a service queue fills, a security team notices anomalies, public rumor spreads, a witness leaves, conditions worsen, or a faction changes its offer. Each tick has a cause and an observable sign when appropriate.

Keep an event schedule with trigger, responsible actor, expected action, dependencies, and alternate behavior if interrupted. NPCs adapt to player actions rather than replaying a script.

## Clues and evidence
Represent a clue with source, content, event time, collection time, integrity, confidence, interpretation, and custody. Separate observation from inference. A log showing an account was used does not prove who controlled it. Make uncertainty legible.

## Technical and cyber scenes
Prepare fictional topology, system purpose, access boundaries, capability gates, abstract vulnerabilities, monitoring, and consequences. A failed attempt should still produce a concrete fictional response: denied access, noisy probe, decoy information, lockout, trace buildup, operator callback, or no useful result. Never require real exploit commands or real targets.

## AI-assisted GM operation
An LLM may propose NPC dialogue, summarize a scene, generate alternatives, classify player intent, or explain fictional system feedback. It must not silently decide canonical truth, change campaign rules, invent authoritative evidence, or execute consequential actions without host approval.

Tag each contribution as proposal, narration, interpretation, sourced fact, or authorized state change. The host decides which categories can affect persistent state.

## LOD and boundary
LOD 0 gives premise and consequence. LOD 1 adds actors and locations. LOD 2 adds causal chains, clues, schedules, and dependencies. LOD 3 adds inventories, maps, rosters, timelines, and system state. LOD 4+ details only mechanisms that matter to play.

This is a GM craft sourcebook, not a rule system. The campaign rules determine checks and resources. SURPS records setting truth and scenario semantics; software packages may implement selected mechanics but are not implied by this document.
