**SURPS sourcebook · Physical storage, custody, and logistics**

## 1. Identity

A warehouse is an organized system for holding goods, materials, resources, or other physical things between acquisition and use. It may be a purpose-built building, a cave, a fenced yard, a ship's hold, a caravan, a granary, a temple treasury, a merchant's back room, a communal storehouse, a subterranean vault, or a distributed network of caches.

The defining feature is not architecture or machinery. It is the organized relationship between **stored things, custody, location, condition, and future disposition**.

Warehousing exists wherever a society produces, gathers, trades, inherits, captures, or prepares more of something than it immediately consumes.

## 2. The warehouse through time

These are overlapping patterns, not a universal timeline. Different patterns can coexist in one settlement or civilization.

### Household and communal stores
- Pits, baskets, jars, lofts, cellars, granaries, smokehouses, root cellars, and seasonal caches.
- Household reserves and shared stores maintained by kin groups, villages, monasteries, or other institutions.
- Knowledge is held in custom and memory: who contributed, what is stored, when it was placed, and who may draw from it.
- Risks include moisture, pests, rot, fire, theft, hidden depletion, and unequal access.

### Ancient and classical institutions
- Grain depots, temple stores, royal treasuries, armories, dockside storehouses, caravanserai, and tax collection depots.
- Storage becomes an instrument of taxation, redistribution, military readiness, political legitimacy, and famine response.
- Seals, tallies, tokens, marks, scribes, inventories, and standardized containers extend trust beyond personal memory.
- A storehouse can be both a public safety net and an instrument of extraction or control.

### Medieval and early trade networks
- Merchant warehouses, guild stores, port warehouses, fortified granaries, monastic cellars, caravan depots, and market-day temporary stores.
- Ownership may be separated from physical custody. Brokers, porters, guilds, factors, guards, and scribes create chains of responsibility.
- Bills, seals, ledgers, marks, and trusted witnesses support goods moving farther than their owners can travel.
- Warehouses cluster around roads, rivers, ports, bridges, markets, mines, and political boundaries.

### Industrial and modern systems
- Rail depots, factories' raw-material stores, cold stores, bonded warehouses, distribution centers, container yards, automated high-bay warehouses, and fulfillment centers.
- Mechanization and standardization increase throughput while creating dependencies on power, transport schedules, specialized labor, and centralized coordination.
- Pallets, containers, barcodes, radio-frequency identification, warehouse management systems, conveyors, cranes, and robotics alter how goods are located and moved.
- Just-in-time supply reduces some inventory costs while increasing exposure to transport disruption and synchronized failure.

### Future and speculative systems
- Autonomous dark warehouses, robotic mines, molecular-material banks, orbital depots, asteroid resource stores, self-repairing reserves, and distributed planetary caches.
- Storage may be managed by autonomous agents, synthetic ecologies, or institutions spanning multiple worlds.
- Matter may be stored as raw material, modular components, encoded manufacturing instructions, energy, or recoverable feedstock.
- A warehouse may be a mobile swarm, a network of sealed habitats, or a place whose contents are fabricated on demand rather than physically stockpiled.

## 3. What can be stored?

| Family | Examples | Special concerns |
|---|---|---|
| Food and biological goods | Grain, fruit, meat, seed, medicines, living cultures | Spoilage, pests, temperature, humidity, contamination |
| Raw materials | Ore, timber, stone, fibers, chemicals | Bulk, toxicity, weathering, segregation, handling |
| Manufactured goods | Tools, clothing, machines, components | Damage, obsolescence, serial identity, compatibility |
| High-value goods | Currency, jewels, art, rare materials | Custody, authentication, theft, insurance, controlled access |
| Hazardous goods | Fuel, explosives, corrosives, pathogens | Isolation, containment, legal control, emergency response |
| Living inventory | Livestock, plants, organisms, preserved specimens | Welfare, habitat, disease, feed, life support |
| Strategic reserves | Water, food, fuel, medicine, spare parts | Allocation, rotation, scarcity, political legitimacy |
| Knowledge-bearing artifacts | Books, tablets, maps, samples, records | Preservation, interpretation, authenticity, access |

A setting may add unfamiliar categories. Define the storage requirements from the properties of the contents rather than forcing every item into modern inventory assumptions.

## 4. Facility anatomy

A warehouse can contain any subset of these functional zones:

- **Receiving:** where goods enter, are unloaded, counted, inspected, and accepted or rejected.
- **Quarantine and inspection:** where uncertain, damaged, contaminated, or restricted goods are isolated.
- **Classification:** where goods receive identity, ownership, category, condition, destination, and handling rules.
- **Storage:** shelves, racks, bins, silos, vaults, yards, pits, tanks, cold rooms, cages, or secure chambers.
- **Picking and staging:** where requested goods are gathered and prepared for dispatch.
- **Dispatch:** where custody transfers to a carrier, customer, institution, or another facility.
- **Records and control:** ledgers, tags, maps, terminals, custodial stations, and audit mechanisms.
- **Support:** power, water, ventilation, drainage, fire protection, repair shops, staff areas, loading access, and waste handling.

These are logical functions, not mandatory rooms. A village granary may combine most of them in one structure; a global logistics network may distribute them across many organizations.

## 5. Operating cycle

A typical inventory lifecycle is:

1. **Acquire** — receive, harvest, manufacture, purchase, collect, or recover.
2. **Identify** — determine what the item is and establish its identity or batch.
3. **Accept** — inspect condition, quantity, provenance, and authority to store.
4. **Locate** — assign a place and record how to retrieve it.
5. **Preserve** — maintain required environmental and security conditions.
6. **Account** — track quantity, ownership, condition, age, and movement.
7. **Retrieve** — locate, select, verify, and remove the requested goods.
8. **Transfer** — dispatch, sell, issue, loan, consume, or move to another store.
9. **Reconcile** — compare recorded stock with physical reality.
10. **Retire** — repair, recycle, return, destroy, or dispose of unusable goods.

A simple facility may perform these steps informally. A complex facility may assign each step to different staff, machines, or institutions.

## 6. People, power, and institutions

Potential roles include owner, steward, quartermaster, warehouse keeper, receiver, porter, picker, packer, guard, inspector, appraiser, clerk, engineer, mechanic, driver, customs officer, auditor, scavenger, and emergency responder.

The social model matters:
- Who owns the goods?
- Who controls access?
- Who bears loss when inventory is damaged or missing?
- Can the facility refuse an order?
- Are reserves public, private, sacred, military, or communal?
- Is inventory visible to the population, the state, the owner, or nobody?
- Who decides allocation during famine, war, disaster, or blockade?

Storage is often political. Control of a granary, water reserve, fuel depot, or medicine store can matter as much as control of the fields, wells, mines, or factories that supply it.

## 7. Capacity, flow, and constraints

Useful domain quantities include:
- usable volume and floor/yard area;
- mass and load limits;
- number and type of storage positions;
- intake and dispatch throughput;
- retrieval latency;
- occupancy and reserve capacity;
- labor and equipment availability;
- environmental-control capacity;
- inventory age, spoilage, and loss rate;
- transport capacity and replenishment delay.

Capacity is not simply volume. A facility may have empty space but lack the right shelf, container, temperature zone, access route, labor, or handling equipment for a particular item.

## 8. Failure, hazards, and conflict

Include mundane failures as well as dramatic events:
- rot, pests, mold, rust, leakage, evaporation, and material degradation;
- fire, flood, storm, earthquake, landslide, extreme heat, and cold;
- mislabeling, misplaced stock, inaccurate ledgers, counterfeit goods, and broken seals;
- theft, fraud, corruption, sabotage, labor disputes, and unauthorized access;
- blocked roads, port closures, fuel shortages, power loss, war, and political seizure;
- overstocking, understocking, bottlenecks, poor rotation, and cascading supplier failures.

Recovery can be more interesting than the initial incident: emergency rationing, salvage, alternate routes, inventory reconstruction, contamination testing, and disputes over responsibility.

## 9. LOD guidance

- **LOD 0:** a store exists; it holds a resource; access and depletion matter.
- **LOD 1:** identify facility type, owner, broad capacity, supply role, access model, and major risks.
- **LOD 2:** define zones, inventory families, workflows, staff roles, transport links, preservation systems, and operating schedule.
- **LOD 3+:** define actual layout, storage positions, individual stock, named staff, equipment, routes, procedures, and encounter events.

Do not detail every crate in a city. Zoom into the warehouse when its contents, operation, staff, or failure materially affects play or simulation.

## 10. Simulation hooks

Possible state variables: capacity, occupancy, stock by category, stock condition, age, ownership, access permissions, throughput, staffing, equipment condition, environmental state, security posture, and replenishment delay.

Possible events: delivery, rejection, misplacement, spoilage, theft, inspection, stockout, emergency issue, dispatch, audit discrepancy, equipment failure, and recovery.

These are domain hooks for a SURPS-enabled simulation to select from. They are not claims that this repository currently simulates warehouses.

## 11. Relationship to DataWarehouse

The software library's terms — lake, shelf, slot, handle, alive state, and dirty state — are implementation vocabulary for a particular in-memory storage model. They may be used as analogies in this sourcebook, but they are not universal warehouse mechanics and should not be imposed on physical-world facilities.

The SURPS warehouse domain describes physical storage and logistics. The DataWarehouse library remains a separate technical product.
