# SURPS Facility Atlas — Named Sites Across Time and Space

**SURPS sourcebook · LOD 3–4 facility profiles · Scenario-ready locations**

> **Use note:** Every named facility in this atlas is a constructed setting example. Capacities, dimensions, staffing, schedules, and incidents are illustrative scenario values, not historical measurements or claims about real sites. Historical settings are inspired by broad, well-attested operating patterns; adapt details to the specific culture and period.

## 1. How to use the atlas

The archetype catalogue describes reusable patterns. These profiles demonstrate how to turn a pattern into a coherent place with geography, custody, physical layout, people, resource flows, dependencies, and failure consequences.

A profile is not a universal blueprint. Change its scale, technology, customs, ownership, or operating assumptions to fit the campaign. Preserve causal consistency: a facility's capacity must fit its space and handling methods; its services must have dependencies; and its failures must affect something that people value.

### Profile detail levels

- **LOD 3 — Operational site:** named zones, routes, roles, stock categories, normal procedures, dependencies, and incident conditions.
- **LOD 4 — Encounter-ready site:** individual access points, local schedules, key personnel, current stock state, active incidents, and actionable choices.

Numbers marked *scenario value* are adjustable dials. They should not be mistaken for empirical facts.

## 2. The Reed-and-Clay Granary of Namar

**Archetype:** communal granary / temple-state storehouse  
**Era and setting:** bronze-age-inspired river valley; a fictional settlement  
**LOD:** 3, with LOD 4 encounter locations  
**Primary purpose:** carry seed and staple grain from harvest through the dry season and distribute emergency rations.

### Site and layout

The granary occupies a raised compound above the floodplain, within a short guarded walk of the threshing ground and canal landing. Its perimeter wall has one public issue gate and one controlled receiving gate. The compound is divided into:

1. **Receiving court:** carts unload here; loads are weighed with standard measures and checked for damp, stones, and pests.
2. **Tally porch:** scribes record household, field, measure, quality, and seal marks on clay tablets or equivalent local records.
3. **Drying yard:** grain awaiting acceptance is spread and turned; bad weather creates a queue and spoilage risk.
4. **Store rooms:** raised floors, thick walls, small controlled openings, and separate bins distinguish seed grain from food grain.
5. **Offering chamber:** a small, separately accounted store used for ritual obligations; its contents are not automatically available for rationing.
6. **Issue court:** authorized households collect measured allotments; disputes are heard at a public bench.
7. **Watch room:** guards control keys and seals but do not independently alter the ledger.

### Operating cycle

At harvest, deliveries are sampled before acceptance. Accepted grain is assigned to a bin by crop, quality, harvest, and intended use. A portion is retained as seed; another portion is reserved for institutional obligations; the remainder supports ordinary and emergency distribution. First-in-first-out rotation is attempted, but seed lots are kept separate even when older.

The granary steward reconciles physical measures against tally records at each major issue. The scribe records obligations; the gatekeeper records movement; the steward authorizes release. This separation is imperfect but creates witnesses.

### People and authority

- **Granary steward:** accountable for stocks and allocation.
- **Scribe:** maintains tallies, receipts, debt, and ration records.
- **Measure keeper:** maintains the standard measures and witnesses disputes.
- **Laborers:** unload, dry, clean, move, and inspect grain.
- **Guards:** protect gates and seals.
- **Priestly or civic authority:** defines ritual claims and emergency priorities.

The ruler may claim ownership, but households may understand the grain as a communal survival reserve. This disagreement is a political fault line, not a bookkeeping error.

### Scenario state

Illustrative starting state: 8,000 local volume-units in accepted grain; 1,200 units earmarked as seed; 500 units reserved for institutional obligations; 6,300 units available for ordinary and emergency issue. A wet harvest can reduce accepted stock before the ledger is closed.

### Failure and encounter hooks

- The ledger reports a full bin whose floor has begun to sink.
- A seal is intact but the grain behind it has been replaced with lower-quality stock.
- A flood warning forces the steward to choose between moving seed grain and feeding a displaced neighborhood.
- The measure keeper discovers that the official measure has been altered.
- A rival authority offers immediate food in exchange for control of the issue gate.

**Simulation hooks:** harvest arrival, quality inspection, drying delay, pest loss, stock rotation, ration authorization, household demand, flood level, and competing claims.

## 3. The Seven Wells Caravanserai Depot

**Archetype:** route depot / mobile-cache network  
**Era and setting:** fictional desert trade corridor with pre-industrial transport  
**LOD:** 3  
**Primary purpose:** keep people, pack animals, and high-value cargo alive between reliable water sources.

### Site and layout

The compound is built around a protected well court. A second, smaller well lies outside the main wall and is used only when the primary draw is unsafe or overloaded. The site includes:

- **Arrival apron:** animals and carts are counted before entering.
- **Water court:** draw order is scheduled; separate troughs serve people and animals.
- **Fodder loft:** dry fodder is stored above ground level, away from the animal pens.
- **Merchant bays:** lockable rooms hold cargo under named custodians.
- **Repair shed:** tack, wheels, axles, containers, and simple tools are maintained.
- **Message room:** route reports, credit notes, and sealed dispatches are held for the next carrier.
- **Sleeping rooms and infirmary:** travelers are separated from sick animals and quarantined cargo.
- **Outer cache markers:** small reserves lie along the route, but their positions are known only to trusted route keepers.

### Service model

The depot sells water, fodder, repairs, storage, security, and information. Payment may be coin, barter, credit, service, or a treaty obligation. A merchant can own cargo while the depot accepts responsibility only for sealed custody. A lost seal triggers an investigation, not automatic compensation.

The route keeper tracks water availability and travel time to the next stop. The caravan master decides whether to proceed, wait, split the convoy, or hire local guides.

### Capacity and constraints

Illustrative scenario values: 60 travelers, 90 pack animals, and 12 carts can be supported during a normal stop. Water is the hard constraint. Fodder can be rationed; water cannot be replaced by better accounting. A failed well changes the facility from a commercial stop into an emergency refuge.

### Failure and encounter hooks

- The well's water level falls despite lower traffic.
- A cache marker has been moved, but the route keeper's map is correct.
- A merchant claims a sealed bay held more cargo than the intake witness recorded.
- A sandstorm delays a convoy until fodder reserves are critical.
- The depot's protection contract conflicts with a local tribe's claim to the water source.

**Simulation hooks:** well yield, draw rate, animal demand, travel legs, weather, cache discovery, cargo custody, repair queues, and route reputation.

## 4. The Deep Ledger of Khar-Dûm

**Archetype:** dwarven deep vault / fortress store  
**Era and setting:** fantasy subterranean hold  
**LOD:** 4  
**Primary purpose:** sustain a mountain community during siege while protecting irreplaceable tools, records, seed, and strategic materials.

### Site and layout

The Deep Ledger is not one chamber. It is a chain of vault districts connected to the hold's service galleries:

1. **Upper receiving hall:** goods from the surface are inspected before entering the protected interior.
2. **Civic stores:** food, cloth, lamp fuel, and daily-use tools are issued on a rotating schedule.
3. **Water cistern galleries:** separated reservoirs reduce single-point contamination; channels have inspection gates.
4. **Forge reserve:** alloy stock, charcoal substitutes, refractory materials, and spare machine parts are held near workshops but isolated from heat and sparks.
5. **Seed and culture vault:** viable seed, fungal cultures, medicinal stocks, and breeding records receive dedicated environmental care.
6. **Record vault:** treaties, lineage records, maps, engineering plans, and debt ledgers are copied onto durable media and stored in separate chambers.
7. **Deep seal:** a restricted district contains dangerous, sacred, or politically contested materials. Its access protocol requires multiple custodians.
8. **Emergency galleries:** handcarts and marked routes connect refuge areas to water, food, and ventilation controls.

### Operations and governance

Each district has a keeper. The Ledger-Master reconciles district accounts but cannot open the Deep Seal alone. The hold's council sets reserve thresholds; the military commander may request emergency issue but cannot silently reclassify civic stores as military property.

The ventilation guild monitors airflow, smoke, humidity, and heat. The water guild maintains cisterns and drainage. The forge master reports material consumption and critical part shortages. These guilds have authority because their systems keep the stores usable.

### Failure model

The main risk is not simply running out of food. It is losing the infrastructure that makes stored resources accessible: blocked galleries, ventilation failure, groundwater intrusion, fire in a forge reserve, or a compromised cistern. A store that cannot be reached is functionally unavailable.

### Active scenario

A lower gallery begins taking water. The leak is slow enough to permit investigation but fast enough that one store district must be evacuated within two days. The affected passage also carries the only handcart route to a machine-part reserve. The council must choose between sealing the gallery, opening a politically restricted bypass, or diverting labor from the surface defenses.

### Encounter locations

- **The Measure Door:** a sealed entry whose stone counterweights reveal whether a store has been opened.
- **The Quiet Cistern:** a reservoir whose water is clear but has an unexplained metallic taste.
- **The Unlisted Shelf:** a record chamber omitted from the public vault map.
- **The Sump Gallery:** a maintenance corridor where water, tools, and evidence accumulate.

**Simulation hooks:** reserve days, air quality, water integrity, gallery accessibility, structural condition, labor allocation, guild authority, and access quorum.

## 5. The Municipal Record House of Bellweather

**Archetype:** civic archive / scriptorium  
**Era and setting:** fictional industrial-era port city  
**LOD:** 3  
**Primary purpose:** preserve deeds, maps, court records, shipping registers, tax rolls, and public correspondence.

### Site and layout

The archive occupies a stone-and-brick civic building near the courts but above the most flood-prone waterfront streets. Public service rooms face the square; restricted stacks occupy the interior; receiving and conservation work happen in a rear wing.

- **Public search hall:** catalogues, reading desks, supervised consultation.
- **Registry counter:** intake of deeds, permits, court filings, and official copies.
- **Sorting room:** records are checked, assigned identifiers, and routed to departments.
- **Map room:** oversized plans and charts are stored flat or on purpose-built supports.
- **Bound-volume stacks:** ledgers and books are arranged by department and date.
- **Loose-record stacks:** boxes and bundles retain original order where it carries evidentiary meaning.
- **Conservation room:** cleaning, repair, drying, and controlled handling.
- **Secure evidence room:** records under court seal or legal hold.
- **Basement plant and stores:** heating, pumps, cleaning supplies, and fire equipment; flood exposure makes this zone a known weakness.

### Custody and service

The archive preserves records on behalf of institutions and citizens; it does not own every record it holds. Access may be public, restricted by law, temporarily sealed, or limited to staff. Certified copies require a traceable chain from original to copy.

A finding aid can be wrong even when the record survives. Staff therefore maintain provenance, accession history, and cross-references. A missing catalogue entry is not proof that an item never existed.

### Staff

The city archivist manages policy and public trust. Registrars accept records. Cataloguers describe and locate them. Conservators stabilize fragile materials. Porters move boxes under controlled supervision. Court officers and municipal departments can request sealed material but must document access.

### Failure and scenario hooks

- A street-level flood threatens the basement pumps while a rare map is being conserved nearby.
- Two departments submit contradictory copies of the same deed.
- A box is returned with its seal intact but its contents reordered.
- A new administration demands the destruction of records still under legal hold.
- A fire drill reveals that the evacuation plan assumes a stairway that is now blocked by construction.

**Simulation hooks:** accession queue, retrieval delay, environmental condition, legal restriction, provenance chain, conservation backlog, fire/flood status, and public access demand.

## 6. The North Quay Cold-Chain Exchange

**Archetype:** industrial cold store / transshipment warehouse  
**Era and setting:** fictional early-to-mid twentieth-century industrial port  
**LOD:** 3  
**Primary purpose:** transfer perishable cargo between ships, rail, and urban distribution while preserving temperature-sensitive goods.

### Site and layout

The facility is organized around temperature zones rather than ownership:

- **Dock receiving:** cargo is checked against manifests and temperature history.
- **Quarantine bay:** damaged or suspect consignments are isolated.
- **Chilled rooms:** goods requiring cool but non-frozen storage.
- **Frozen rooms:** deep-cold storage with insulated doors and controlled traffic.
- **Ice / refrigeration plant:** mechanical plant, coolant handling, maintenance access, and monitoring.
- **Rail dispatch platform:** insulated loading path to refrigerated railcars.
- **Packing and grading floor:** sorting, repacking, weighing, and labeling.
- **Fuel and spares store:** protected from the cold rooms and separated from ignition sources.
- **Drainage and washdown system:** manages meltwater, sanitation, and waste.

### Operating logic

Every consignment has an owner, a custodian, a declared product class, an acceptable temperature range, and a dispatch deadline. The warehouse may accept cargo without guaranteeing its market value; it guarantees only the agreed handling and custody conditions.

The plant operator reports system condition separately from the warehouse manager's stock ledger. This prevents a full inventory report from hiding a failing refrigeration system.

### Bottlenecks and risks

The bottleneck is often transfer time at doors and loading platforms, not nominal room volume. Each open door admits heat and moisture. A delayed train can force a choice between continued storage, rerouting, or controlled disposal.

### Incident scenario

A refrigeration compressor fails during a heat wave. The backup unit can maintain only two of the four cold zones. One zone contains high-value cargo; another contains essential medical supplies; a third contains food owned by a cooperative. The facility manager must allocate cooling under a public contract and a private insurance agreement that disagree.

**Simulation hooks:** zone temperature, door-open duration, compressor state, backup capacity, cargo sensitivity, dwell time, loading schedule, spoilage threshold, and disposal authority.

## 7. Meridian Fulfillment Campus

**Archetype:** automated fulfillment center / industrial maintenance store  
**Era and setting:** contemporary-to-near-future metropolitan logistics region  
**LOD:** 4  
**Primary purpose:** receive diverse goods, hold inventory, assemble orders, and dispatch them to local and regional carriers.

### Campus anatomy

The campus comprises multiple buildings with separate traffic and fire boundaries:

1. **Inbound yard:** appointment slots, trailer staging, seal inspection, and unloading.
2. **Induction building:** barcode/RFID identification, dimensioning, weighing, and exception handling.
3. **Reserve storage:** pallet racks and bulk zones for slower-moving inventory.
4. **Automated storage and retrieval system:** dense storage for compatible containers.
5. **Goods-to-person picking:** robotic delivery of bins to staffed or automated pick stations.
6. **Manual exception mezzanine:** irregular, fragile, oversized, or poorly identified goods.
7. **Packing and sortation:** order consolidation, labeling, carrier routing.
8. **Outbound docks:** staging lanes by carrier and departure window.
9. **Maintenance shop:** robot spares, conveyor components, batteries, sensors, and tools.
10. **Control room and local compute:** operational dashboards and local fallback controls.
11. **Power and charging yard:** utility feeds, battery systems, charging, and emergency power.
12. **People facilities:** training, rest, safety, first aid, and shift handover.

### System behavior

Inventory truth is distributed across physical scans, control software, exception queues, and human corrections. A successful scan does not prove an item is physically present; a robot's reported location can be stale; and an order can be logically complete while its carton remains trapped at a jammed conveyor.

The campus therefore needs reconciliation procedures and safe manual modes. Staff must be able to isolate a conveyor segment, recover a misplaced item, and continue limited operations without defeating safety interlocks.

### Scenario values and constraints

Illustrative scenario values: 180,000 active item locations, 24,000 order lines per peak hour, 18 outbound carrier windows per day. These values are a campaign scale setting, not a benchmark or engineering specification.

Peak performance depends on labor availability, dock capacity, sortation rate, charging, software health, carrier arrival patterns, and exception frequency. Expanding storage without expanding dispatch can increase congestion rather than throughput.

### Incident scenario

A routing service begins assigning orders to a conveyor loop whose downstream diverter is offline. The physical line keeps moving, creating a growing recirculation queue. The control room can halt the entire loop, isolate one section, or switch to a reduced manual dispatch plan. Each option sacrifices a different service promise.

**Simulation hooks:** order arrival, inventory confidence, robot availability, conveyor utilization, exception rate, labor shifts, energy demand, dock appointments, and service-level penalties.

## 8. Asterion Polar Edge Station

**Archetype:** remote edge station / scientific data observatory  
**Era and setting:** near-future polar research outpost  
**LOD:** 3  
**Primary purpose:** collect environmental observations, preserve local records, and provide autonomous computation during long communication gaps.

### Site and layout

The station is a compact cluster rather than one building:

- **Instrument field:** weather, ice, seismic, and atmospheric sensors.
- **Instrument service bay:** calibration standards, replacement probes, and repair benches.
- **Habitat:** sleeping, food, medical supplies, and sanitation.
- **Power module:** generation, batteries, fuel reserve, and load shedding.
- **Thermal plant:** heat distribution, waste-heat recovery, and freeze protection.
- **Data room:** local storage, compute, network equipment, and offline copies.
- **External cache:** emergency food, fuel, tools, and survival equipment.
- **Landing zone:** cargo and evacuation interface; access depends on weather and visibility.

### Autonomy contract

The station is designed to operate for 90 days without resupply (scenario value). It can continue essential sensing and local analysis during a communications outage, but it cannot promise immediate remote support. The local director can shed nonessential compute loads to protect life support and core instruments.

Raw observations, calibration records, derived products, and operator notes are stored separately. A result without its calibration and provenance is treated as provisional.

### People and operations

A small rotating team includes station lead, instrument scientist, field technician, systems engineer, medic, and logistics specialist. In practice, roles overlap. A maintenance task may compete directly with sleep, field safety, or scientific coverage.

### Incident scenario

A storm damages an external sensor mast and blocks the landing zone. The station's data room is healthy, but one instrument's calibration expires before the next possible repair window. The team must decide whether to continue publishing provisional measurements, suspend that instrument, or attempt a hazardous field repair.

**Simulation hooks:** days of autonomy, fuel, battery state, heat margin, sensor health, calibration age, communication latency, crew fatigue, and weather access window.

## 9. Selene Archive One

**Archetype:** lunar knowledge core / distributed planetary archive  
**Era and setting:** speculative lunar settlement  
**LOD:** 4  
**Primary purpose:** preserve the settlement's technical, ecological, legal, and cultural memory while supporting local operations during Earth communication delays or outages.

### Site and layout

The archive is embedded in a shielded service volume near—but not inside—the habitat's primary life-support plant. Its systems are divided into independent compartments:

1. **Public knowledge terminal:** local reference material and civic records.
2. **Operations repository:** verified maintenance procedures, habitat models, fabrication instructions, and emergency checklists.
3. **Scientific archive:** raw instrument data, metadata, calibration histories, and derived products.
4. **Cultural collection:** oral histories, language recordings, art, education, and community records.
5. **Cold replica vault:** offline copies on media selected for long-term retention and periodic migration.
6. **Local compute bay:** indexing, simulation, validation, and settlement services.
7. **Provenance office:** records the origin, revision, authority, and validation state of consequential documents.
8. **Recovery console:** isolated tools for rebuilding indexes and restoring services without depending on Earth.

### Authority and trust

The archive is not the settlement's sovereign. It stores records under policies established by civic, scientific, and operational authorities. A technical procedure can be marked as draft, tested, approved, superseded, or withdrawn. A historical record can be preserved even when its claims are disputed.

No single administrator can silently rewrite the authoritative record. High-consequence changes require signed review by the relevant authority and an auditable history. This is a governance rule in the scenario, not a claim about the DataWarehouse library.

### Continuity model

The settlement maintains three distinct copies: an online working copy, a local offline recovery copy, and a periodically exchanged external replica. The external replica may lag by days or weeks. A network partition therefore creates version conflicts that must be resolved by provenance and authority, not simply by choosing the newest timestamp.

### Incident scenario

After a long communications blackout, an Earth-delivered archive update arrives with a later timestamp but an incomplete signature chain. It changes a pressure-door maintenance procedure. The local engineering team has a validated older procedure and a new sensor anomaly. The archive can preserve both records, but the settlement must decide what is authoritative and what action is safe.

**Simulation hooks:** replica age, integrity checks, provenance status, approval quorum, local storage margin, energy budget, media migration schedule, and communication delay.

## 11. Turning a profile into a live scenario

For any selected site:

1. Establish its normal operating state before introducing a crisis.
2. Identify one scarce resource and one hard dependency.
3. Assign each critical subsystem an owner or responsible role.
4. Define what the facility knows, what it merely assumes, and what it cannot observe.
5. Create an incident that propagates through at least two dependencies.
6. Give the participants choices with different costs; avoid a single obvious correct response unless the scenario is instructional.
7. Track consequences in the same units the facility uses: measures of grain, water volume, temperature, staff-hours, service windows, data integrity, or autonomy days.

### Suggested state categories

- **Physical:** quantity, condition, location, accessibility, environmental state.
- **Operational:** throughput, queue, schedule, equipment availability, maintenance.
- **Institutional:** ownership, custody, authority, restrictions, obligations.
- **Informational:** identity confidence, provenance, version, integrity, replication.
- **Human:** staffing, skill, fatigue, trust, morale, local knowledge.
- **Environmental:** weather, terrain, contamination, structural condition, external access.

These are domain-level modeling categories. A SURPS implementation may select only the variables needed for a scenario. They are not a schema requirement for the DataWarehouse .NET library.

## 12. Relationship to the software repository

This atlas describes places and operating systems in fictional and historical settings. It does not define a universal record layout, allocator, page model, persistence format, concurrency policy, or public API for the DataWarehouse library. The library's current code and tests remain the authority for its actual software behavior.
